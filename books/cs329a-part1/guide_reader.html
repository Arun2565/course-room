Course overview for Stanford CS329A on self-improving AI agents: five years of scaling trends, what bigger models unlocked, the ChatGPT leap, inference-time scaling, reasoning models, and where chatbots end and agents begin. Condensed from the lecture transcript into a study guide.

Why it matters

Almost every idea in this course is an answer to one question: models already know far more than a single answer reveals, so how do we draw it out? In the lecture's own framing, we will start with an overview of large language models, then turn to what the course covers. Scaling laws explain the first wave of progress. Post-training explains ChatGPT. Inference scaling and reasoning models explain the current wave. Agents are what happens when all of it is pointed at a goal instead of a chat box. If you can say how each stage changed what models can do, the rest of the course slots into place.

Scaling laws: compute, data, parameters

Since GPT-3, one pattern has held: as you scale up parameters, compute, and data, test loss falls. More compute on the x-axis, lower loss on the y-axis. Bigger datasets, lower loss again. More layers and parameters, lower loss a third time. That predictable fall is what built GPT-3, ChatGPT, PaLM, and Gemini.

The growth was exponential:

But the foundation held only until recently: scaling was starting to hit a saturation point, and small models performing well cuts against the naive reading of the laws. The laws describe loss, not everything a model can do.

What bigger models unlocked

Size bought three things loss curves never predicted. First, few-shot learning: give the model a task description plus a few examples and it follows the template, no fine-tuning needed. Zero-shot is the same with no examples at all, like translating a word it was never explicitly taught to translate. Second, reasoning appears only in larger models: capabilities that are absent at small sizes and arrive suddenly past a threshold.

Chain-of-thought is the most durable of these. Instead of showing question-answer pairs, you show the worked steps: Roger has five tennis balls, buys two cans of three, so 6 new plus 5 is 11. Small models around 7 billion parameters gain nothing from this. Large ones learn the process from context and apply it to new problems, including modular arithmetic and word unscrambling. That property still underlies reasoning models today.

Chain-of-thought was not designed in. Researchers gave models hard problems, added reasoning chains, and watched them do better. GSM8K showed the first signs of life; PaLM showed it was a big deal.

The ChatGPT leap

ChatGPT launched in November 2022 and reached a million users in five days. The leap over GPT-3 was not parameter count. It was two additions: instruction tuning and RLHF.

The pipeline has four stages. Pre-training predicts the next token over internet-scale text and is the easy step. Then fine-tuning continues next-token prediction on much higher-quality data, books and essays companies pay millions for, and the model becomes much better as a result. Then instruction tuning teaches question-answering and following instructions, including chain-of-thought traces, from human-written and templated pairs. Finally RLHF replaces labels with a reward model built from human ratings of answers and steers the model toward generations the reward model scores highly, weighting correctness, helpfulness, specificity, and harmlessness as desired.

Inference-time scaling

A year and a half ago the frontier moved again: the model stays fixed and extra compute goes into inference. The Large Language Monkeys work asked the model to solve each problem not once but up to 10,000 times, then used a verifier, unit tests for code, to pick a correct answer from the pile.

The result: small models that lose to GPT-4o at one sample each beat GPT-4o once samples scale up. The models already knew more than single-shot answers suggested. On the hardest problems only three or four of 10,000 samples were correct, which shows both the hidden capability and why scaling inference matters. Parallel samples cost wall-clock little but real money, and diversity needs temperature tuning: past about 1.2 the output turns to gibberish. Without verifiers the same idea needs LLM judges or reward models, which get a lecture of their own.

Inference scaling means no parameter changes. Test-time compute, verifiers, and sampling strategy do the work that training used to do.

Reasoning models

DeepSeek in December 2024 and the o-series showed the next combination: bringing fine-tuning and test-time scaling together generates synthetic training data, math solutions checked against known answers, coding solutions checked by tests, and fine-tunes the model on it. That loop is the self-improving piece the course is named for.

Reasoning models work the way people do on hard problems: analyze the problem, decompose it into addressable subtasks, try something, check feedback from tests or tools, self-correct, and backtrack to a different approach when stuck. The o1 traces show this progression explicitly, and unlike prompted chain-of-thought the model produces the thinking itself. Measured on hard math, accuracy rises log-linearly with test-time compute, the same shape training scaling used to have. These models beat GPT-4o at math, analysis, and programming, though not at personal writing, and they curiously prefer their own traces to better ones from other models.

From chatbots to agents

Chatbots and reasoning models are still single-turn or chat-format: fun, but they accomplish no task. Agents close that loop with a goal, a plan, actions on an environment, feedback, and a stopping rule. Deep Research can survey sites and return pros and cons of where to live for a year. Coding agents edit files, run terminals, and fix tests from English instructions.

Most real systems today are agentic workflows, hand-built graphs rather than open loops: prompt chaining for decomposed subtasks, routing for complexity-dependent paths, parallelization with aggregation for research, an orchestrator LLM that plans, evaluators that judge, and verifiers that check. The ingredients per step are LLM calls, tool calls like search, critics, and unit-test-style verification in verifiable domains such as math and code.

Where agents already pay: repetitive coding work like migrations and restructuring, customer support with transcription and knowledge assist and smart replies, deep research reports that replace manual literature reviews, and AI-scientist loops that brainstorm ideas, iterate experiments, and draft writeups. Verification remains the bottleneck, and the generator-verifier gap, easy to generate, hard to confirm, decides where self-improvement is possible at all.

Worked example

Track one hard math problem through the lecture's numbers.

  1. Ask once. A small model answers; odds are it fails where GPT-4o succeeds.
  2. Ask 10,000 times at moderate temperature. Coverage, problems solved by at least one sample, climbs past GPT-4o for the small model.
  3. Inspect the pile. On the hardest problems only 3 or 4 of the 10,000 verify correct, so without a verifier the capability stays hidden.
  4. Spend the loop wisely. Parallel samples dodge latency but not cost; temperature past about 1.2 destroys diversity quality.
  5. Close the loop. Verified traces become synthetic training data, fine-tuning lifts single-shot accuracy, and the next round starts from a stronger model. That is inference scaling feeding train-time scaling.

Key takeaways

  • Loss falls with compute, data, and parameters, but loss curves never predicted few-shot learning, emergence, or chain-of-thought.
  • ChatGPT's leap was post-training: high-quality fine-tuning, instruction tuning, and RLHF on human-rated answers.
  • Inference scaling extracts hidden capability: sample many times, verify, keep what passes. Small models can beat giants this way.
  • Reasoning models merge both scalings: test-time traces become synthetic training data, which is the self-improvement loop.
  • Agents add goals, plans, environment actions, and feedback to the chat loop; verification quality decides where they work.
  • The generator-verifier gap is the binding constraint: generation is cheap, trustworthy feedback is not.

Common mistakes

  • Reading scaling laws as "bigger is always better" and missing the saturation point and the small-model counterevidence.
  • Treating chain-of-thought as prompt garnish rather than the learned reasoning process that training now optimizes.
  • Sampling at high temperature for diversity and getting gibberish instead; the useful band tops out near 1.2.
  • Expecting RLHF to fix data problems; fine-tuning quality dominates before preferences matter.
  • Calling a chained prompt an agent; without goals, environment actions, and a stopping rule it is a workflow.

Practice

  • Reproduce the scaling table from memory with all five rows and explain what each jump unlocked.
  • Write the four-stage post-training pipeline and say what data each stage consumes.
  • Take one IMO-style problem and describe how you would spend 1,000 samples plus a verifier on it, including the cost-latency tradeoff.
  • Sketch an agentic workflow for a codebase migration: planner, tool calls, verifier, and where a human checks in.
  • Run the o1-style loop on paper for a small coding task: analyze, decompose, attempt, test, correct, backtrack once.

Interview questions

  1. What do scaling laws say, and what do they fail to predict?
  2. Walk through pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, instruction tuning, and RLHF: what changes at each step?
  3. Why can repeated sampling let a small model beat a larger one, and what role does the verifier play?
  4. What distinguishes a reasoning model from a base model with chain-of-thought prompting?
  5. Define an agent versus a chatbot, and name the loop that connects them.
  6. What is the generator-verifier gap, and in which domains does it block self-improvement?
